## Market Analysis

The bull case rests on elevated baseline conditions: ocean temperatures in the Atlantic Main Development Region have been running 0.5-1°C above historical averages since 2023, and the transition from La Niña to neutral/weak El Niño conditions typically reduces Atlantic wind shear, favoring intensification. The 2024 Atlantic hurricane season produced Category 5 hurricanes (Beryl, Milton), establishing that the atmospheric machinery for rapid intensification exists in this climate state. With roughly 40 months remaining until expiry, two full hurricane seasons will pass—statistically, the US experiences a Category 5 landfall approximately once every 10-15 years on average, though clustering does occur (2004-2005 saw multiple landfalls within months). The most vulnerable window is August-October in both 2025 and 2026, when sea surface temperatures peak and African tropical waves have maximum fetch.

The bear case emphasizes that Category 5 landfalls remain legitimately rare: only four have hit the continental US since 1851 (Camille 1969, Andrew 1992, Michael 2018, and Milton 2024). Even during the hyperactive 2005 season, only Katrina (barely Category 3) and Wilma made landfall at that intensity. Gulf coast positioning becomes critical—storms recurving toward the open Atlantic miss entirely, and even slight wind shear increases or dry-air entrainment can drop intensity from 5 to 4 quickly. El Niño's arrival, if strengthening, would increase Atlantic shear and work against the bull thesis.

Traders should monitor sea surface temperature anomalies (reported weekly by NOAA) and the August 2025 Atlantic hurricane season forecast (released late May 2025) as primary catalysts. Real-time tracking begins June 1 each year when the official season starts. Any Category 5 Atlantic basin formation should trigger immediate repricing, regardless of landfall probability, since the technical definition is unambiguous. Watch for La Niña redevelopment signals after mid-2025, which would meaningfully extend odds by suppressing Atlantic shear through 2026.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is "Category 5 hurricane" defined, and could borderline cases create ambiguity?

The Saffir-Simpson scale uses sustained wind speeds (157+ mph for Category 5), which are measured by aircraft reconnaissance and satellite, so classification is objective at the time of landfall—however, storms can intensify or weaken in final hours before striking, creating edge cases where pre-landfall measurements vs. landfall-moment measurements might differ slightly.
