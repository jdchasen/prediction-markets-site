This matters because the Strait handles roughly 20-25% of global oil trade, and any sustained disruption would trigger immediate energy price spikes and economic consequences—making the market a proxy for whether traders expect escalation in US-Iran tensions, regional conflicts, or sanctions enforcement over the next 18 months.

The bull case rests on historical baseline traffic patterns: the Strait regularly sees 50-100+ transits daily during normal conditions, meaning 20 ships on any single day is a modest threshold unlikely to be breached unless conflict severely disrupts operations. Conversely, any de-escalation signals—diplomatic talks, sanctions rollback, or reduced military posturing—would push odds higher.

Yes, during the 1980s Iran-Iraq War tanker traffic collapsed dramatically, but in the post-2000 era normal daily transits range 50-100+, making sub-20 days rare outside active military conflict or formal blockades.

### If a blockade occurred for just 48 hours, would that trigger market resolution against the YES side?

### How sensitive is this market to oil price movements versus actual geopolitical events?

Oil prices can rise on supply-disruption fears without actual Strait closures occurring, so traders should distinguish between perceived risk (priced into crude futures) and realized operational disruption (what this market measures).
