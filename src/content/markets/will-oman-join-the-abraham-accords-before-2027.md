## Market Analysis

The current 16.5% YES pricing suggests markets view Oman's accession to the Abraham Accords as unlikely but plausible within the four-year window, reflecting genuine diplomatic uncertainty rather than consensus dismissal. This matters now because regional normalization efforts have stalled since the initial 2020 wave of signings, and Oman's potential participation could either accelerate broader Gulf reconciliation or remain indefinitely shelved depending on U.S. diplomatic priorities and Iran dynamics.

The bull case rests on several tangible factors: Oman has historically served as the region's diplomatic mediator and maintains pragmatic non-aligned status that makes normalization politically easier than for more exposed Gulf states; the sultanate faces economic pressure from oil-dependent budgets that could incentivize UAE/Saudi economic partnerships; and if the Abraham Accords expand into a genuine multilateral framework rather than bilateral deals, Oman could join without the symbolic costs of first-mover disadvantage. Additionally, a second Trump administration (post-January 2025) would likely prioritize expanding the accords as a foreign policy legacy item, creating institutional momentum through 2026. The bear case is more structural: Oman has successfully avoided formal alignment for decades and leadership under Sultan Haitham shows no urgency to change this posture; public opinion across the Arab world remains hostile to normalization with Israel, constraining political space; Iran's regional influence and Oman's geographic proximity create genuine security concerns about appearing overly aligned with the U.S.-Gulf bloc; and the absence of active negotiations or leaked discussions suggests accession isn't currently on any diplomatic agenda. The accords also no longer carry the novelty premium they did in 2020, reducing incentive structures for late signers.

Key catalysts to monitor through 2026 include: any major U.S.-brokered regional peace initiative announcements (likely mid-2025 if pursued by Trump administration), Gulf Cooperation Council summits in May and November annually where normalization could surface, Israeli-Palestinian developments that might trigger regional backlash against further accords signings, and economic data on UAE-Oman trade that could signal deepening commercial interdependence. Watch also for statements from Oman's Foreign Ministry or Sultan Haitham in diplomatic forums—silence itself is informative. The December 31, 2026 expiry means the market prices in roughly 20 months of remaining diplomatic activity, making this essentially a 2025-26 event bet rather than a true four-year window.
