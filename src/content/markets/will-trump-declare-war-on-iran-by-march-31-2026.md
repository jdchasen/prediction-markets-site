## Market Analysis

The bull case centers on several catalysts: Iran's nuclear program could reach breakout capacity within months, potentially forcing Trump's hand on military action he's previously threatened. Bolton-style hawks in Trump's administration might push for confrontation if Iran attacks U.S. interests or Israeli targets escalate tensions. Trump declared in his first term he came "very close" to striking Iran in 2019, and a second term with fewer institutional constraints could lower the threshold for military action. The March 2026 timeline captures the critical first 14 months when presidential action faces fewer midterm political constraints.

The bear case is straightforward: the United States hasn't formally declared war since 1942, with presidents from both parties conducting military operations under authorization for use of military force (AUMF) or claimed Article II powers instead. Even major conflicts in Iraq, Afghanistan, Libya, and Syria proceeded without declarations. Congressional Republicans would likely support military strikes but resist an actual declaration that could constrain executive flexibility and create political accountability. Trump himself favored transactional deals over prolonged military commitments in his first term, nearly securing negotiations with Iran in 2019-2020.

Key factors to monitor include Iran's uranium enrichment levels reported quarterly by the IAEA, any Iranian attacks on U.S. forces in Syria or Iraq, and Trump's cabinet appointments—particularly Secretary of State and Defense. The House and Senate Armed Services Committees' 2026 calendar will signal whether war authorization debates gain traction. Traders should watch for rhetoric shifts around Iran's nuclear timeline, currently estimated at weeks to weapons-grade material if political decisions are made. The probability appears rationally low given historical precedent, though genuine military conflict without formal declaration remains considerably more likely.
