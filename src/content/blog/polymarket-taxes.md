[Polymarket](https://polymarket.us/1762?utm_source=masterpredictionmarkets&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=signup) does not send you a 1099. It does not report your trades to the IRS. And it does not calculate your cost basis for you. But if you made money trading prediction markets on Polymarket, you owe taxes on those gains — and the IRS is paying more attention to crypto-based income than ever.

Because Polymarket runs on the Polygon blockchain and settles in USDC, your tax situation is meaningfully different from trading on a regulated exchange like [Kalshi](/blog/kalshi-tax-reporting). The burden of tracking, calculating, and reporting falls entirely on you. This guide walks through exactly how to handle it.

**Disclaimer:** This is educational content, not tax advice. Tax law around crypto prediction markets is evolving. Consult a CPA or tax professional for advice specific to your situation.

## The Big Difference: No 1099, No Safety Net

When you trade on [Kalshi](/blog/kalshi-review), you get a 1099-B at the end of the year showing every trade, your cost basis, and your gains. The IRS gets a copy too. It is straightforward — the numbers match, you report them, you are done.

Polymarket gives you none of that. There is no 1099-B. There is no annual tax summary. There is no cost basis calculation. You are responsible for tracking every single transaction yourself.

This does not mean your gains are invisible to the IRS. The IRS has blockchain analytics tools and has been actively pursuing unreported crypto income. The question is not whether they can find your Polymarket activity — it is whether they choose to look. Given the billions in volume flowing through the platform, the odds of increased scrutiny are rising.

## How Polymarket Gains Are Taxed

The IRS has not issued specific guidance on crypto prediction markets, but the most widely accepted treatment is that Polymarket gains are taxed as **capital gains on digital assets**. Here is how that works.

### Short-Term vs. Long-Term

- **Held less than one year**: Short-term capital gain, taxed at your ordinary income rate (up to 37% federal)
- **Held more than one year**: Long-term capital gain, taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income

Since most prediction market contracts settle within days or weeks, nearly all your Polymarket gains will be short-term. That means they are taxed at your full income tax rate.

### No Section 1256 Treatment

Unlike Kalshi, which is CFTC-regulated and may qualify for Section 1256's favorable 60/40 tax split, Polymarket is not a regulated exchange. Your trades do not qualify for Section 1256 treatment. Every gain is classified purely by holding period, and since most contracts are short-term, you are paying your full ordinary rate.

This is a real cost difference. A trader in the 32% bracket making $10,000 on Polymarket pays roughly $3,200 in federal taxes. The same gains on Kalshi under Section 1256 would be approximately $2,300. That is a $900 difference on the same profit.

| | Kalshi (Section 1256) | Polymarket |
|---|---|---|
| Tax treatment | 60% long-term / 40% short-term | 100% short-term |
| $10,000 gain at 32% bracket | ~$2,300 | ~$3,200 |
| 1099 issued | Yes | No |
| Cost basis calculated | By exchange | By you |

### Could It Be Gambling Income?

Some tax professionals argue that prediction market gains should be classified as gambling income rather than capital gains. Under gambling tax treatment, your wins are taxed as ordinary income and your losses are only deductible if you itemize and only up to the amount of your winnings.

This is a worse outcome than capital gains treatment for most traders, because capital losses can be deducted more broadly. The IRS has not definitively ruled on this classification for crypto prediction markets. Most traders and CPAs treat it as capital gains on digital assets, but this is an area where professional advice matters.

## Tracking Your Trades: What You Need

Since Polymarket does not provide tax forms, you need to build your own records. Here is exactly what to track for every trade.

### For Each Position

| Field | Example |
|-------|---------|
| Date acquired | 2026-01-15 |
| Market | "Will the Fed cut rates in March 2026?" |
| Side | YES |
| Shares purchased | 200 |
| Price per share | $0.42 |
| Total cost (USDC) | $84.00 |
| Gas fees | $0.15 |
| **Cost basis** | **$84.15** |
| Date settled/sold | 2026-03-19 |
| Settlement/sale price | $1.00 (settled YES) |
| **Proceeds** | **$200.00** |
| **Gain/Loss** | **+$115.85** |

### Where to Get Your Data

1. **Polymarket account**: Your trade history shows buys, sells, and settlements with timestamps and amounts.
2. **Polygonscan**: Your wallet address on Polygon shows every on-chain transaction. Search your wallet address to see all USDC movements.
3. **Crypto tax software**: Tools like Koinly, CoinTracker, or TokenTax can import wallet transactions and help calculate cost basis. Some now support Polymarket activity specifically.

### The USDC Complication

Trading on Polymarket requires USDC, which means you have an additional layer of transactions to track:

1. **Buy USDC** on a centralized exchange (Coinbase, Kraken, etc.)
2. **Transfer USDC** to your Polygon wallet
3. **Deposit USDC** into Polymarket
4. **Trade** on Polymarket
5. **Withdraw USDC** from Polymarket
6. **Transfer USDC** back to an exchange
7. **Sell USDC** for USD

Each step involving a crypto asset is technically a taxable event. However, since USDC is a stablecoin pegged to $1.00, the gain on holding USDC itself is typically negligible. Most CPAs treat USDC transfers at par ($1.00 = $1.00) and focus the tax analysis on the prediction market trades themselves.

If USDC ever depegs significantly while you hold it, the gain or loss on the stablecoin itself would also need to be reported. This is unlikely but worth noting.

## Reporting on Your Tax Return

Polymarket gains are reported using the same forms as other capital gains:

### Form 8949

List each trade as a separate line item:
- **(a)** Description: e.g., "Polymarket - Fed Rate Cut March 2026 YES"
- **(b)** Date acquired
- **(c)** Date sold or settled
- **(d)** Proceeds (settlement payout or sale price)
- **(e)** Cost basis
- **(h)** Gain or loss

Since Polymarket does not issue a 1099-B, check **Box C** (short-term, no 1099) or **Box F** (long-term, no 1099) for each trade.

### Schedule D

Transfer your totals from Form 8949 to Schedule D, which summarizes your overall capital gains and losses for the year.

### High Volume Traders

If you made hundreds of trades, listing each one individually on Form 8949 is impractical. You can attach a summary statement with trade-level detail and report the totals on Form 8949. Crypto tax software generates these summary statements automatically.

## Deducting Losses

If you had a losing year on Polymarket, those losses have tax value:

- **Offset capital gains**: Polymarket losses can offset gains from stocks, crypto, other prediction markets, or any other capital asset
- **Deduct against income**: If net capital losses exceed gains, deduct up to **$3,000 per year** against ordinary income
- **Carry forward**: Unused losses carry forward indefinitely to future tax years

This makes tracking losing trades just as important as tracking winners. Every losing trade reduces your tax bill.

One caveat: if your gains are classified as gambling income rather than capital gains, your losses can only offset gambling winnings and only if you itemize deductions. This is another reason to discuss classification with a tax professional.

## Gas Fees and Transaction Costs

Gas fees paid on Polygon for Polymarket transactions are added to your cost basis, reducing your taxable gain. These are typically small (a few cents per transaction on Polygon), but they add up over hundreds of trades.

Track gas fees for:
- Depositing USDC into Polymarket
- Each buy and sell transaction
- Withdrawing USDC from Polymarket

If you bridge funds from Ethereum to Polygon, the bridging gas fee on Ethereum (which can be several dollars) is also part of your cost basis.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Assuming No 1099 Means No Taxes
The most common and most dangerous mistake. The IRS does not care whether Polymarket sends you a form. All income is reportable. With blockchain analytics becoming more sophisticated, the risk of getting caught is real and growing.

### 2. Only Tracking Winning Trades
Your losses are worth money at tax time. If you only track your winners, you overpay on taxes. Track every trade, win or lose.

### 3. Ignoring USDC On-Ramp and Off-Ramp
If you buy USDC at $0.999 and sell it later at $1.001, that is technically a taxable gain. In practice, most CPAs treat USDC at par, but you should maintain records of your exchange transactions in case the treatment is ever questioned.

### 4. Mixing Personal and Trading Wallets
Use a dedicated wallet for Polymarket trading. Mixing personal crypto transactions with prediction market activity makes record keeping exponentially harder and increases audit risk.

## When to Get Professional Help

Talk to a CPA if any of these apply:

- Your Polymarket gains exceed $5,000
- You trade on multiple platforms or exchanges
- You are unsure whether to classify gains as capital gains or gambling income
- You have significant losses to deduct
- You are a non-US resident trading on Polymarket
- You used leverage or complex trading strategies

A crypto-savvy CPA costs $200-$500 for a consultation. Given the lack of clear IRS guidance on crypto prediction markets, professional advice is especially valuable here. The cost is deductible as a tax preparation expense.

## The Bottom Line

Polymarket does not make your tax life easy. There is no 1099, no automatic cost basis, and no clear IRS guidance on classification. But the obligation is real, and the IRS is getting better at tracking crypto income every year.

The smart approach: use a dedicated wallet, track every trade, run your transactions through crypto tax software, and consult a CPA if your activity is substantial. The traders who treat their [prediction market activity](/blog/what-are-prediction-markets) as a legitimate financial endeavor — including the unglamorous tax part — are the ones who avoid nasty surprises from the IRS.
